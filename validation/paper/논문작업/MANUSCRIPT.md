## Abstract

APEX (Automated Photometry EXtraction) is a desktop application that takes a non-specialist, through a graphical step-by-step workflow and without scripting, from raw CCD frames to a science product: a star-cluster colour-magnitude diagram or a multi-night variable-star light curve. It composes standard detection and photometry algorithms behind a graphical interface backed by a Qt-free, reproducible core, filling a gap between powerful command-line suites with a steep entry barrier and graphical tools scoped to single-field or transit photometry. Because the contribution is accessibility rather than a new algorithm, we validate the shared measurement chain in several independent layers, each checked against something the pipeline did not itself produce. On self-contained synthetic data the detector's completeness function is well behaved (50 per cent depth measured to $\pm0.08$ mag) and the reported uncertainties propagate correctly (pull standard deviation $1.014$). Two independently implemented engines agree with APEX to milli-magnitude precision, SEP on synthetic truth (MAD $0.006$ mag) and IRAF/DAOPHOT on a real 278-star frame (MAD $0.004$ mag). The pipeline's colour-magnitude diagram reproduces those of two independent instruments (main-sequence ridgeline agreement $19$ mmag); a faint-end anomaly is traced to a Gaia BP reference artifact rather than an APEX error; and re-reduction of two globular clusters shows no crowding-dependent bias between aperture and PSF photometry down to the resolution the data reach. APEX also performs its own detector calibration (bias, dark, and flat): the output is bit-identical to two independent reduction pipelines and reproduces frames from two further cameras, so a reduction can run raw-to-science. We state explicitly what is not validated: automated isochrone-parameter recovery, photometry against strongly structured backgrounds, sub-resolution blending, and the cross-instrument generality of the photometry, whose real-data validation uses a single camera (the calibration is validated across three).

**Keywords:** techniques: photometric – methods: data analysis – software: development – open clusters and associations: individual: NGC 457, NGC 6811 – globular clusters: general – stars: variables: $\delta$ Scuti

## 1. Introduction

Watching photometric software simply work leaves an impression. A student running exoplanet-transit photometry in a guided graphical tool such as HOPS \citep{tsiaras2019}, and obtaining a usable light curve in an afternoon, comes away expecting that turning a stack of CCD frames into calibrated stellar brightnesses is a solved, push-button task. For several common observing programmes on small telescopes, it is not yet that simple, and the gap between the expectation and the available tooling is the motivation for this work.

Two mature families of photometry software dominate practice. The first is the lineage of command-line reduction suites: DAOPHOT for crowded-field stellar photometry \citep{stetson1987} and SExtractor for source extraction \citep{bertin1996} remain standard, capable, and widely trusted. Their power comes at the cost of a steep entry barrier. Installation is nontrivial, much of the reference documentation predates current builds, and progress stalls at the many points where a parameter is ambiguous and the newcomer has no principled way to set it. The second family is the modern scientific-Python stack, where `photutils` \citep{photutils} on top of Astropy \citep{astropy2013, astropy2018, astropy2022} offers flexible, well-tested primitives. That flexibility presumes the user can write the script that orchestrates them. Between these sits a set of graphical tools that trade generality for guidance, but each is scoped to a particular problem: AstroImageJ \citep{collins2017} and HOPS \citep{tsiaras2019} are built around single-field differential and transit photometry, while variable-star packages such as Munipack/MuniWin \citep{hroch2014} and VStar \citep{benn2012} target time-series analysis and are, in the authors' experience, difficult to approach without prior familiarity.

No widely used graphical tool covers the whole path from raw frames to two of the most common small-telescope products: a star-cluster colour-magnitude diagram (CMD) and a multi-night variable-star light curve. Both require the same measurement chain — detection, an astrometric solution, a master source catalogue, and forced photometry at fixed positions — before any science-specific step. The existing tools do not provide that shared chain together with a guided route through it.

APEX (Automated Photometry EXtraction) is built to fill this gap. It is a desktop application that carries a user, through a graphical step-by-step workflow, from image selection to either a cluster CMD or a variable-star light curve. Two operational modes share a common measurement chain and then branch: a CMD mode for cluster photometry and a light-curve (LC) mode for multi-night differential photometry and period analysis. The shared chain performs source detection, WCS plate solving, master-catalogue construction, and forced aperture and PSF photometry, using standard algorithms drawn from the lineage above rather than new ones. A deliberate architectural choice separates the graphical layer from a Qt-free computational core, so that every reduction step can also be run headlessly and reproducibly; the same core that backs the interface is exercised, unchanged, in the validation experiments of this paper.

Because APEX composes established algorithms rather than introducing new ones, its contribution is not algorithmic novelty. It is accessibility paired with demonstrated trustworthiness, in the spirit of automated-pipeline papers such as AutoPhOT \citep{brennan2022}. Concretely, this paper contributes: (i) a description of an integrated, graphical, end-to-end photometry pipeline that reaches cluster-CMD and multi-night light-curve science products without scripting; and (ii) a multi-layer, reproducible validation of its shared measurement chain, in which every accuracy claim is checked against something the pipeline did not itself produce, an independent measurement engine, an independent reference catalogue, or an injected known truth. We regard the second contribution as inseparable from the first: modern AI-assisted development has lowered the barrier to *building* scientific software as much as graphical tools lower the barrier to *using* it, and that shift makes independent, reproducible validation not an optional flourish but the precondition for trusting such a tool at all.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes APEX's design and the shared measurement chain in enough detail to interpret the validation. Section 3, the core of the paper, presents the validation suite: detection completeness, the photometric error model, parameter and observing-condition sensitivity, cross-checks against the independent engines `sep` and IRAF/DAOPHOT, automatic frame quality control, a cross-catalogue reference test, reproduction of the cluster CMD across independent photometric systems, and a crowded-field test in two globular clusters. Section 4 demonstrates the pipeline end to end on a variable-star science case. Section 5 discusses what the validation does and does not establish, including its single-instrument scope and the problems deliberately left out of scope, and Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Design and implementation

### 2.1 Architecture

APEX is organised around a deliberate split between a graphical presentation layer and a Qt-free computational core. The graphical layer, built on PyQt5, presents the workflow as an ordered sequence of step windows and owns every interactive element: file selection, crop definition, the sky-preview image viewer, and the mode-specific analysis steps. The numerically substantial steps, source detection, astrometric solution, and forced photometry, live in a separate core (`apex.analysis`, orchestrated by `apex.pipeline`) that imports no Qt and can be driven headlessly. This is not an aspirational refactor. The same core functions that back the interface are called directly, unchanged, by the benchmark and validation harness used throughout Section 3, so that what is validated is exactly what the interface runs. A headless pipeline runner plans the step sequence, checks prerequisites, executes steps idempotently, and writes a JSON run manifest; that runner is what makes the reproductions of Section 3 possible.

Two operational modes share the first seven steps and then branch. CMD mode continues to cluster-specific analysis (PSF photometry, calibration, the colour-magnitude diagram, and isochrone modelling); LC mode continues to differential light-curve construction, multi-night detrending, and period analysis. Because the shared chain is what this paper validates, we describe it in detail and the mode branches only briefly.

A second choice shapes how the rest of the paper reads. Wherever an established library covers a step, APEX uses it — photutils and Astropy for detection, aperture and PSF photometry, WCS handling, and periodograms — and it writes its own numerical code only where no library provides the needed function. Four such cores remain: the per-frame aperture-correction workflow (photutils supplies curve-of-growth primitives, but not the automatic reference-star selection and application), phase-dispersion minimization (absent from `astropy.timeseries`), the SYSREM detrending algorithm (not in a standard library), and an optional built-in plate solver that removes the external-solver dependency. This split — library-backed steps versus a small set of self-implemented cores — is what organises the validation of Section 3: the library-backed steps are checked through their end-to-end output, and the self-implemented cores are checked directly, the built-in solver against the ASTAP and astrometry.net solutions it can be run alongside.

### 2.2 The shared measurement chain (Steps 1–7)

APEX performs its own detector calibration as an optional first stage (Step 0). It builds master bias, dark, and flat frames (median or sigma-clipped combine), or accepts pre-built masters, and applies them as $\mathrm{calibrated} = ((\mathrm{raw}-\mathrm{bias}) - k\,\mathrm{dark})/\mathrm{flat} + \mathrm{pedestal}$, with optional overscan trimming and cosmic-ray/hot-pixel cleaning (L.A.Cosmic). It re-implements the science-legitimate subset of standard bias/dark/flat reduction — sharing code heritage with the authors' AstralImage imaging tool, disclosed here as provenance — and deliberately excludes imaging-only stages that are unsafe for photometry (colour-filter-array demosaicing, frame coaddition, local normalisation, and row/column defect flattening); APEX targets mono science CCDs. This stage is validated in Sections 3.11–3.14, and a reduction may therefore begin from raw frames or from externally calibrated frames. Steps 1–3 prepare the data. FITS files are selected and their headers scanned to resolve the target and instrument, a common crop region is defined on a reference frame and applied to the stack, and a sky-preview step provides interactive inspection together with an automatic frame quality-control evaluation (validated in Section 3.6).

Source detection (Step 4) runs a selectable detection engine: the SEP library \citep[the SExtractor backend;][]{bertin1996}, photutils image segmentation \citep[`detect_sources`/`deblend_sources`;][]{photutils, astropy2022}, or `DAOStarFinder`, with three presets (normal, crowded, faint) setting the parameters. Sources are detected above a configurable threshold in units of the background RMS. The validation in this paper uses the SEP engine (at $3.2\sigma$); the detector is a single implementation shared by the interface and the headless core.

Astrometric calibration (Step 5) solves each frame's world-coordinate system with a local ASTAP solve \citep{astap}, used as the primary engine because it runs entirely offline and returns a solution fast enough to process a full night unattended; when ASTAP does not converge the pipeline falls back automatically to a local astrometry.net solve \citep{lang2010}, whose blind quad-matching is slower but more robust. A solution is not trusted on faith. Each solve is verified by matching detected sources to Gaia \citep{gaia_dr3_2023} and recording the residual statistics — matched-star count, match and inlier rates, and the sigma-clipped residual RMS — and it is rejected unless it clears fixed thresholds (at least 20 matched stars, residual RMS below 2.5 px, and a 99th-percentile residual below 5 px). A surviving solution is refined by shifting the reference pixel to cancel the median source-to-Gaia offset, and the residuals are written into each frame's FITS header for audit. Coordinate transformations use `astropy.wcs` \citep{astropy2013}. Step 6 merges the per-frame detections into a single master source catalogue, which fixes the positions at which every frame is subsequently measured.

Forced aperture photometry (Step 7) measures every master source in every frame at its catalogue position. Fluxes are integrated in a circular aperture with fractional-pixel weighting and the sky is estimated in a concentric annulus, using `photutils` apertures \citep{photutils}. The default aperture radius scales with the frame's measured FWHM, $r_{\rm ap} = \max(4\,{\rm px},\, 0.8\,{\rm FWHM})$, and the sky annulus spans roughly 4–6 FWHM; all radii are configurable, and Section 3.4 examines the aperture choice empirically. Uncertainties follow the full CCD equation, combining source and background Poisson noise, background-estimation error, and read noise; the honesty of these reported uncertainties is tested in Section 3.3. Photometry is vectorised over the sources within a frame and parallelised over frames, with a worker count set by the host machine (75 per cent of cores, bounded above), so that a night of several hundred 16-megapixel frames reduces in a single pass.

A fixed aperture sized for signal-to-noise captures only part of a star's light, and the captured fraction drifts with the seeing, so each frame is corrected to a common total-flux scale. APEX builds this aperture correction from a growth curve of bright, isolated stars on the frame itself and applies the resulting factor to every source's flux and uncertainty; the per-frame value and its reference-star count are logged, so the correction is auditable rather than opaque. We do not benchmark it in isolation, but because the independent cross-checks of Sections 3.5 and 3.6 compare final, aperture-corrected magnitudes, their milli-magnitude agreement also bounds any error it introduces.

Instrumental magnitudes are defined as a count rate, $m_{\rm inst} = 25 - 2.5\log_{10}(F_e / t_{\rm exp})$, which is exposure-time invariant. Absolute calibration (in CMD mode, Step 10) proceeds from these instrumental magnitudes through a per-frame zeropoint and an optional colour term to an absolute scale anchored on Gaia DR3 \citep{gaia_dr3_2023} reference stars, with astrometric and photometric quality cuts (the RUWE index and the corrected BP/RP flux-excess factor $C^*$; \citealt{riello2021}). Frame-to-frame offsets and atmospheric extinction are separated by projecting the frame-offset basis to remove the component degenerate with airmass.

### 2.3 Mode-specific analysis

After Step 7 the two modes diverge. In CMD mode, an optional PSF-photometry step (Step 8) builds an empirical point-spread function from bright, isolated stars in each frame using the `photutils` EPSF machinery and remeasures sources by PSF fitting; when it is skipped, downstream steps fall back to the forced-aperture results. Calibration (Step 10), the colour-magnitude diagram (Step 11), and isochrone modelling (Step 12) follow. In LC mode, the user selects target and comparison stars (Step 8), builds differential light curves (Step 9), detrends and merges nights (Step 10, using the SYSREM algorithm; \citealt{tamuz2005}), and searches for periods (Step 11, by Lomb–Scargle and phase-dispersion minimization; \citealt{stellingwerf1978}). This paper validates the shared chain and, in Section 3.8, the CMD product; isochrone-parameter recovery and LC-mode period recovery are treated separately (Sections 4 and 5.2), and we are careful not to present the mode-specific steps as validated beyond what Sections 3 and 4 actually establish.

### 2.4 Reproducibility and configuration

All runtime parameters are read from a single TOML file, keeping instrument, detection, solving, and photometry settings in one auditable place. Pipeline state, which steps have run and with what data, is persisted as JSON, and every step's outputs are written to canonical paths derived from a small set of path helpers rather than assembled ad hoc, so that a result tree is self-describing and re-enterable. Cached intermediate products (header scans, detections, photometry) carry a signature and a schema version and are invalidated when a governing parameter changes, which prevents stale results from surviving a configuration change. Together these make a reduction reproducible from the parameter file and the input frames alone, the property on which Section 3 depends.

## 3. Validation

### 3.1 Approach and data

Every result in this section checks APEX against something it did not itself produce: an injected known truth, an independently implemented measurement engine, or an independent reference catalogue. No script fits a free parameter to the data it then judges; zeropoints and colour terms are always solved on an independent bright-star subset before residuals are examined at the faint end. We include the one investigation that turned up a genuine discrepancy (Section 3.8) precisely because a validation exercise that reports only confirmations is not a validation.

Two kinds of experiment appear below. The self-contained synthetic experiments (Sections 3.2–3.5 and 3.7) render a population of stars over a Poisson-plus-Gaussian noise background with a realistic FITS header, entirely in `numpy` and `astropy` \citep{astropy2013, astropy2022}, from APEX's own synthetic-frame generator with a fixed seed and no external data. Artificial-star injection uses an empirical PSF extracted from the frame itself and reruns APEX's production, Qt-free detector, not a toy re-implementation. The real-data cross-checks (Sections 3.6, 3.8–3.10) use previously reduced APEX result trees for the open clusters NGC 457 and NGC 6811 and the globular clusters M5 and M13, each re-run through the current headless code immediately before its figure was produced, so all real-data results reflect the current codebase rather than an archived one. One caveat, verified from FITS headers rather than assumed, governs the whole section: every real dataset used here was taken with a single camera (Section 5.3).

Table 1 records which of APEX's internally built components the experiments below actually test, and how directly. We distinguish three cases: *direct*, where an independent engine, catalogue, or injected truth checks the component's own output; *indirect*, where the component is exercised inside a downstream product that is itself checked; and *not yet*, where the component is implemented and instrumented but not validated against truth in this paper. We prefer to mark a gap than to imply coverage we do not have. For the zeropoint the calibration-model residual is measured directly — the per-calibrator scatter is $0.03$–$0.08$ mag (robust MAD-$\sigma$) across bands in the reductions used here — and the calibrated magnitudes additionally reproduce the reference CMD (Section 3.9); the extinction solver recovers a known coefficient through the airmass degeneracy on synthetic data, but its fitted value on real data is not checked against an expected extinction; and the cross-frame matching and the necessity of the linear colour term are the two components exercised but not validated against truth here.

### 3.2 Detection completeness

Completeness quantifies how the detector's recovery fraction falls with magnitude. It sets the effective depth of any catalogue APEX builds and shows whether recovery is limited by signal-to-noise or by a magnitude-dependent detector bias. Following the artificial-star completeness test introduced with DAOPHOT \citep{stetson1987}, stars injected across a magnitude ladder and recovered by the production detector — here the SEP engine at $3.2\sigma$ above the background \citep{bertin1996} — give a well-behaved completeness function (Fig. 1a). Recovery is set by a source's signal-to-noise against the background, not by magnitude directly: the recovered fraction is a clean threshold transition in S/N (Fig. 1b) that follows the error-function detection-probability form expected for a flux measured against Gaussian background noise, $\beta = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[1 - \mathrm{erf}(z/\sqrt{2})\right]$ with $z$ the source's significance above the detection threshold \citep{masci2011, kashyap2010} — the same limiting-magnitude model the AutoPhOT pipeline adopts \citep{brennan2022}. The recoveries collapse onto this curve with a 50 per cent point near $\mathrm{S/N}\approx7.4$ (in aperture-photometry S/N); this sits above the nominal $3.2\sigma$ per-pixel threshold because SEP requires a source to clear that threshold over a minimum connected area. The binned recovery fractions are the measurement and the fitted curve only summarises them, its parameters bootstrapped over the 12 injection trials and not re-fit to the binned points. Mapped through the $\mathrm{S/N}(m)$ relation this gives the magnitude completeness of Fig. 1a: a 50 per cent depth $m_{50} = 17.56^{+0.08}_{-0.07}$ mag (bootstrap 95 per cent CI $17.48$–$17.63$), transition width $0.33$ mag, from 840 injections, with completeness above 90 per cent brighter than $m_{90}=16.83$ and below 10 per cent fainter than $m_{10}=18.28$. The few-per-cent shortfall from unity at the brightest bins is a separate, magnitude-*independent* loss (injections landing on defective pixels or close blends) that the detection model does not absorb and does not need to: it leaves the recovered depth and width unaffected.

### 3.3 Photometric error model

On a well-separated grid injected across $m=14.5$–$20.0$ and re-measured, the uncertainties reported by the photometry match the recovered scatter (Fig. 2). The bias is consistent with zero to $m\approx18.5$; the per-bin RMS follows the CCD relation $\sigma_m = 1.0857/{\rm SNR}$ across two decades; and the pull $(m_{\rm meas}-m_{\rm true})/\sigma_m$ for SNR $>5$ is a unit Gaussian (mean $-0.15$, standard deviation $1.014$, $N=3404$). Reported and empirical uncertainties track along $y=x$ to a median ratio of $0.98$, with a mild $\sim$15 per cent under-estimate only at the faintest bins, attributable to the nonlinear magnitude transform near the detection floor. This experiment establishes that the pipeline *propagates* its assumed noise model correctly; that the assumed model matches a real detector is a separate question, supported indirectly by the real-data cross-checks below (Sections 3.6, 3.9), where the measured scatter between APEX and an independent engine is consistent with the reported uncertainties.

### 3.4 Parameter and observing-condition sensitivity

The pipeline and the observing conditions move the metrics in the directions physics predicts, and the trends show which effect limits performance in each regime (Fig. 3). Photometric scatter as a function of aperture radius traces a shallow minimum: it holds near 0.060 mag for radii up to the FWHM, bottoms at 0.058 mag at $1.2\times$FWHM, then climbs to 0.12 mag by $2.5\times$FWHM as the enlarged aperture admits more sky noise than added stellar signal. Position RMSE stays at 0.154 px across every aperture, because the centroid comes from detection, not from the aperture. Sky brightness and seeing both erode depth. As the sky rises from 50 to 1000 ADU the 50 per cent depth shallows from 18.48 to 17.11 mag, because a brighter background lifts the shot-noise floor and pushes faint sources below the detection limit, and the per-bin scatter grows from about 0.05 to 0.15 mag. As the seeing worsens from 2.5 to 6.0 px FWHM the depth shallows from 18.09 to 16.79 mag, because the same flux spread over more pixels lowers the peak signal-to-noise. Both scatter trends rise from endpoint to endpoint but are not strictly monotonic bin to bin, so we quote the endpoints rather than smooth curves. A companion sweep shows the detector is threshold-robust: across detection thresholds of 2–6$\sigma$ on a clean field the depth is constant to $<0.01$ mag with zero spurious detections, so recovery is set by signal-to-noise, not by the threshold. The empirical optimum near $1.2\times$FWHM is reached in practice by the 4-px aperture floor at typical seeing, even though the nominal default scale is $0.8\times$FWHM (Section 2.2).

### 3.5 Independent-engine cross-check on synthetic truth

Measuring the same 95 well-detected stars in one synthetic frame (known truth) independently with APEX and with Barbary's SEP library (a standalone SExtractor backend that shares no code with APEX; \citealt{bertin1996}) gives photometric-grade agreement after a single zeropoint alignment: MAD $0.0060$ mag, RMS $0.0097$ mag, Pearson $r=0.99995$, with 95 per cent of stars agreeing within 0.02 mag (Fig. 4). Two independently implemented engines therefore return the same fluxes on the same pixels.

### 3.6 Independent-engine cross-check on real data

The strongest accuracy test in this suite measures the same stars, at the same fixed sky coordinates, independently with APEX's forced photometry and with IRAF's `phot` (DAOPHOT, via PyRAF), under matched measurement parameters (Table 2). On the fully-APEX-reduced NGC 6811 $V$ frame — carried from raw through APEX's own Step 0 calibration and measurement chain — the two engines agree over 498 stars to MAD $0.0092$ mag after zeropoint alignment, with RMS $0.0228$ mag, Pearson $r=0.99984$, position RMS $0.0004$ px, and 96 per cent of stars within $0.05$ mag (Fig. 5); the residual is flat with magnitude and grows only into the photon-noise-dominated faint end. Because both engines integrate the identical pixels under identical apertures, sky annuli, gain, and read noise (Table 2), the residual isolates the difference between the two flux integrators, and its milli-magnitude size shows they agree at photometric grade. The same test on a second cluster and band, 278 stars in NGC 457 ($g$; MAD $0.0043$ mag, $r=0.99998$), reaches the same conclusion, so the agreement is not specific to one frame, filter, or field. Because these are real observed data and IRAF shares no code with APEX, this tests on-sky correctness rather than internal self-consistency.

### 3.7 Frame quality control

Run on a synthetic 44-frame night (24 clean frames plus four defect classes of five frames each), the automatic frame-QC module with its shipped default thresholds passes all 24 clean frames with no false positives, fails all five bad-seeing frames, and flags all five bright-sky and all five header-lying noisy-readout frames for review (Fig. 6). The one class it does not catch is grey transparency loss: all five "cloud" frames pass, because a uniform transparency change alters no image-level shape, sky-level, or sky-noise statistic. This is a structural blind spot rather than a defect, and it is the direct motivation for APEX's second-stage, photometry-level transparency check (matched-star frame-offset monitoring), which is positioned to catch exactly this case.

### 3.8 Reference-catalogue cross-validation: a reference artifact, not an APEX error

Cross-matching 1928 NGC 6811 stars to Pan-STARRS 1 \citep{chambers2016}, fully independent of Gaia, separates a *reference* systematic from a *measurement* systematic band by band (Fig. 7). In $B$, the Gaia-transformed reference drifts $+0.022$ mag against PS1 toward faint magnitudes, larger than APEX's own $+0.010$ mag; in $V$ the pattern reverses, with the Gaia reference flat ($+0.007$) and APEX carrying a small $+0.024$ mag residual. The dominant systematic swaps catalogue between bands, which is only possible if the two are independent. In $B$ the culprit is Gaia's BP channel, whose flux is background-dominated and biased for faint blue sources \citep{riello2021}; APEX's own $B$, checked against the independent PS1 scale, is flat to $\sim0.01$ mag. The faint "$B$-band drift" first seen against Gaia is therefore chiefly a reference artifact. The practical lesson is that bright-star Gaia anchoring remains safe for the zeropoint, but faint-end *validation* should use a $G$-based or PS1 reference rather than BP-transformed magnitudes.

### 3.9 CMD reproduction across independent photometric systems

The colour-magnitude diagram, the CMD-mode science product, is validated without fitting anything (Fig. 8). The NGC 6811 Johnson CMD ($V$ vs $B-V$, 1921 stars) from APEX ground-based aperture photometry overlays the Gaia-transformed, space-based reference with sigma-clipped main-sequence ridgelines coinciding to 19 mmag RMS; the same cluster in the independent PS1 system shows the identical morphology. This validates the CMD *product*, not isochrone *fitting*: recovering age, metallicity, distance, and reddening from a single-colour ground-based CMD is a known-degenerate inverse problem (Section 5.2), which we treat separately and do not claim here.

### 3.10 Crowded-field replication in two globular clusters

The synthetic experiments use uncrowded fields; this test extends into two real globular-cluster cores and asks whether the result replicates (Fig. 9). Using APEX's own density-peak finder, M5's core is enhanced $34\times$ and M13's $35\times$ over their field backgrounds, both far above NGC 6811's $11\times$ by the same metric. The probe shown is deliberately Gaia-independent: it compares APEX's own two measurement methods, forced aperture versus PSF photometry, on the same matched star list as a function of nearest-neighbour separation. This is an *internal-consistency* test — it bounds how much crowding can make the two methods disagree, not an external accuracy check — and that framing is what makes it robust here, because M13's archived Gaia cross-match is essentially broken (only 38 of 1347 sources resolve in a live Gaia DR3 query) yet the internal comparison needs no Gaia identity. The binned aperture-minus-PSF medians are flat in both clusters, consistent with zero within $\pm0.02$–$0.04$ mag from the resolution floor ($\sim$10 px, $\sim$4$''$) out to isolated separations. Within the separation this ground-based, $2\times2$-binned instrument resolves, no crowding-dependent disagreement between the two methods is detected, and the null result replicates across two independently reduced fields. Sub-resolution blending finer than this dataset resolves is not and cannot be tested here, and remains open.

### 3.11 Detector calibration (Step 0)

APEX's own detector calibration is validated against controlled truth and against an independent pipeline. On synthetic frames built from known bias, dark, and flat fields, injecting those fields and recovering them through APEX's Step 0 returns the input at the noise floor: the calibrated frame carries a systematic offset of $-0.016$ DN (target $0$) with residual scatter (MAD $3.18$ DN) consistent with the injected Poisson-plus-read noise floor ($\sim3.0$ DN), the master bias and dark are recovered to $\sim1$ DN RMS, and the flat (median-normalised to $1.0000$) removes a $21$ DN optical vignette to $2$ DN, so the calibration arithmetic is correct against a controlled ground truth. On a real M13 frame the calibration removes the optical vignette and fixed-pattern structure (Fig. 10a,b), and the pixel-for-pixel agreement with the community-standard astropy \texttt{ccdproc} on real frames (Section 3.13) confirms the same arithmetic on-sky, independently of any code APEX shares. The optional cosmic-ray and hot-pixel step (L.A.Cosmic; \citealt{vandokkum2001}, via astroscrappy) removes 100 per cent of injected cosmic-ray pixels and 94 per cent of hot pixels while leaving stars untouched (star-core false-positive rate 0.000 per cent, aperture-flux change 0.00 mmag; Fig. 10c). APEX therefore performs detector calibration end to end, not aperture photometry on externally reduced frames.

### 3.12 Detector characterisation from the data

APEX measures the detector's gain, read noise, and dark current directly from its own calibration frames (Fig. 11). The photon-transfer relation of same-level flat-pair differences gives a gain of $0.681 \pm 0.014$ e$^-$/ADU and a read noise of $2.35$ e$^-$, consistent after $2\times2$ binning with the IMX455 laboratory value (\citealt{alarcon2023}); the source-free background versus exposure gives a dark current of $0.0077$ e$^-$/s ($R^2 = 0.998$). The gain is *measured*, not read from the header: the MaxIm/ASCOM \texttt{EGAIN} keyword is a factor of $\approx16$ too small for this camera (a documented 12-to-16-bit ADC left-shift), so it is not used. These measured values anchor the error model of Section 3.3 to the detector's real physics.

### 3.13 Preprocessing cross-check against ccdproc

Each calibration stage is compared pixel-for-pixel against the equivalent operation in astropy \texttt{ccdproc}, the community-standard Python CCD-reduction package, on real Moravian C3-61000 frames (Fig. 12). Master bias, master dark, and all three application steps are bit-identical ($\Delta = 0$); master-flat construction and the full pipeline agree to $6\times10^{-8}$ and $5\times10^{-4}$ DN — four to nine orders of magnitude below the read noise. As with the SEP and IRAF photometry cross-checks, two independently written pipelines returning identical pixels shows the bias/dark/flat arithmetic is implemented correctly.

### 3.14 Cross-instrument calibration

The photometry validation above uses a single camera (Section 5.3); the calibration, by contrast, is validated across instruments (Fig. 13). APEX reduces public raw frames from two different Las Cumbres Observatory cameras and is compared against the archive's BANZAI-processed product, an independent published pipeline. On a QHY600 CMOS camera the whole frame agrees to a uniform $+0.06$ e$^-$ offset with a featureless difference image; on a four-amplifier Sinistro CCD the sky and sources agree to $\approx0.3$ per cent, but the difference shows a four-quadrant pattern — the per-amplifier gain, overscan, and cross-talk assembly that BANZAI performs with dedicated Sinistro handling. The bias/dark/flat arithmetic therefore generalises across detectors; multi-amplifier detector assembly is instrument-specific, and APEX targets single-CCD detectors.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What is established

Across Sections 3.2–3.9, APEX's aperture and PSF measurement, from detection through forced photometry and automatic frame quality control, behaves as a well-calibrated instrument. Reported uncertainties propagate correctly (Section 3.3); two independently implemented engines and an independent real-data reference agree at the milli-magnitude level (Sections 3.5–3.6); pipeline and observing-condition parameters move the metrics in the physically expected directions (Section 3.4); and the resulting colour-magnitude diagram is indistinguishable from what two other instruments produce for the same stars (Section 3.9). The one apparent measurement anomaly investigated in depth, a faint-end $B$-band drift, resolved to a documented weakness of the external reference rather than of APEX (Section 3.8). Extended into two crowded real fields, the pipeline continues to perform correctly and the null crowding result replicates (Section 3.10), with the honest qualifier that this is an internal aperture-versus-PSF consistency bound down to the resolution this dataset reaches, not a proof that aperture photometry is crowding-proof at arbitrary separations.

### 5.2 What is out of scope by construction

Automated isochrone-parameter recovery (age, metallicity, distance, reddening) is not validated here. It is a known-degenerate inverse problem for ground-based single- or dual-colour photometry: unconstrained fits rail toward spuriously metal-poor, short-distance solutions, and even with external priors the metallicity retains a bias absent a spectroscopic or blue-band constraint. This is a property of the astrophysical degeneracy and the fit likelihood (cf. the $\tau^2$ CMD-fitting framework of \citealt{naylor2006}), not a photometric-accuracy statement about the measurements validated here; Section 3.9 accordingly validates the CMD product, not its interpretation. The light-curve mode is exercised end to end in Section 4, and is validated only to the extent that section establishes.

Two components of the measurement chain itself remain validated only in part (Table 1). The extinction estimator is verified on synthetic data, where it recovers a known coefficient through the airmass degeneracy, but its fitted value on real frames is not checked against an expected atmospheric extinction; and the master catalogue's cross-frame matching is instrumented and unit-tested for bookkeeping but not scored for purity or completeness against injected truth. Neither undermines the direct photometric cross-checks of Sections 3.5–3.6, which operate on already-matched sources, but both are natural targets for the same injected-truth treatment applied to detection and photometry here, and we leave them as such.

### 5.3 Limits on generality

**Single instrument for the photometry (verified, not assumed).** Every real dataset used for the *photometry* checks (Sections 3.6, 3.8–3.10) was taken with the identical camera; the genuine independence axes there are the cross-check *methods* (SEP, IRAF/DAOPHOT, Gaia, Pan-STARRS 1), which carry no APEX-camera-specific assumption, not the choice of cluster. The *calibration*, by contrast, is validated on two further cameras against an independent pipeline (Section 3.14). A second telescope and camera carried through APEX's photometry as well remains the strongest open generality test for the measurement chain.

**Structured sky background.** APEX estimates the sky in a sigma-clipped annulus, which assumes the background is locally uniform on the scale of the aperture and annulus. This holds for stars on blank sky and is adequate for the smooth, slowly varying backgrounds of the cluster fields used here, where a gentle gradient is largely absorbed by the annulus median. It fails where the background carries structure on the scale of the aperture itself: bright nebular emission, Galactic cirrus, or a star superimposed on resolved galaxy light. There the annulus mis-estimates the true background beneath the source and the photometry is biased in a position-dependent way. This regime is neither used nor validated here, and it is distinct from the stellar crowding of Section 3.10, which is source confusion rather than diffuse background. Photometry of variables or transients embedded in host-galaxy or strong nebular emission would require spatially resolved background modelling or template subtraction, as AutoPhOT performs for transients in galaxies \citep{brennan2022}, which APEX does not currently do. The variable-star target of Section 4 is a Galactic field pulsator in a comparatively clean field, so this limitation does not affect the results reported here.

**Sub-resolution crowding.** Section 3.10 finds no crowding-dependent bias down to the $\sim$10 px ($\sim$4$''$) separation this ground-based, $2\times2$-binned dataset resolves. Finer, sub-resolution blending, as would be probed by space-based or lucky imaging, remains untested and is exactly where aperture photometry is still expected to degrade relative to simultaneous PSF fitting.

**A broken external cross-match need not mean broken measurement.** M13's archived, older-pipeline Gaia cross-match is essentially unusable for absolute calibration (only 38 of 1347 detected sources resolve in a live Gaia DR3 query), yet it remained fully usable for the internal, Gaia-independent aperture-versus-PSF test of Section 3.10, since forced photometry needs only master-catalogue pixel positions, not a Gaia identity.

### 5.4 Practical recommendations

- Use an aperture near $1.0$–$1.2\times$FWHM; the shipped default is already close to the empirical optimum (Section 3.4).
- Treat frame QC as two-stage: the image-level checks validated in Section 3.7 cannot detect grey transparency loss, so a photometry-level (matched-star flux) check is required for that failure mode.
- For faint-end photometric *validation* specifically (not zeropoint anchoring), prefer a $G$-based or Pan-STARRS reference over BP-transformed Gaia magnitudes (Section 3.8).
- In crowded fields, prefer an internal aperture-versus-PSF diagnostic over Gaia-matched calibrator statistics, since Gaia's own quality cuts remove the worst blends before they are counted (Section 3.10).
- In fields with strong nebulosity or resolved galaxy light, prefer local background modelling or template subtraction; the annulus sky estimator is intended for stellar fields (Section 5.3).

## 6. Conclusion

By the evidence assembled here, APEX's shared photometric measurement chain is a validated, honestly-erred, cross-checked instrument for aperture and PSF photometry of point sources, from uncrowded open-cluster fields to real globular-cluster cores, and its CMD-mode product reproduces what independent instruments see for the same stars. APEX further calibrates its own frames from raw, a stage validated bit-identically against two independent reduction pipelines and, across two additional cameras, against a published survey pipeline (Sections 3.11–3.14). This is a claim about *measurement*, deliberately scoped away from automated isochrone-parameter recovery, from photometry against strongly structured backgrounds, from sub-resolution blending, and from instruments not yet re-verified against the current code. The contribution of APEX is to make that validated measurement chain reachable, through a graphical workflow, by observers for whom the established command-line tools have been out of reach, and to have shown, rather than asserted, that the accessibility need not cost accuracy within the regime established here.
